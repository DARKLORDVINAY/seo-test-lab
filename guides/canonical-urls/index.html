Guide

A preferred address needs a reason

A canonical declaration identifies a preferred URL for a page or a group of equivalent pages. It is useful when several addresses expose substantially the same content. Reading the declaration tells you what the publisher requested; it does not prove which address a search engine selected.

Compare the declaring page with its target. Does the target exist? Does it describe the same information? Is the address on the intended host and protocol? A declaration pointing to an unrelated article can create a conflict between the content, links, and stated preference. Treat that conflict as a reason to inspect publisher intent.

Keep related signals consistent

Internal navigation and sitemaps should normally use the addresses that the publisher wants people and crawlers to find. Consistent choices make the site easier to reason about. A redirect, by contrast, moves a request to a different destination; a canonical declaration does not move the reader away from the current page.

Before changing a preference, consider any legitimate duplicate variants, language versions, pagination, tracking parameters, or historical migrations. The correct decision depends on the site's actual organisation. A mechanically different URL is not automatically an error, and a self-referencing declaration is not proof that a page deserves indexing.

Record an observation

Store the requested address, final address, canonical target, observation time, and relevant content comparison. Keep the distinction between the observed declaration and your conclusion about whether it is appropriate. That separation makes a later review or rollback much easier to justify.
